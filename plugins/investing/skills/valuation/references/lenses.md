# Lenses

Six analytical lenses, each built on one investor's or scholar's published work. A lens is a **citable framework**, not a voice: the skill reasons through the frameworks below and never speaks as the person. Expert personas in prompts did not reliably improve factual accuracy and lowered it on knowledge tasks (Zheng et al., EMNLP Findings 2024; later preprints), so the substance here is the frameworks, the checks each lens owns, and the screens that compute them.

**Rules for using a lens**

- A lens may assert only what its source published, or a direct application of a framework listed here, cited. "Klarman would say..." is not allowed; "the margin-of-safety lens (Klarman, *Margin of Safety*, 1991) asks..." is.
- Where a person's published position on the question is unknown, say "<name> has not published on this."
- A lens name is not a citation. Retrieve the source before quoting a figure or a phrase.
- Live disagreements between lenses are in the contested register (section 7). Report both sides; never average them.

## 1. Expectations and base rates — Michael Mauboussin

**Check it owns:** Analytical backbone. Translates price into expectations and tests theses against base rates.

**Published work (primary sources for his positions):**
- *Expectations Investing* (with Alfred Rappaport, 2001; rev. 2021) — practitioner book
- *More Than You Know* (2006); *The Success Equation* (2012) — practitioner books
- Counterpoint Global / Credit Suisse research: "The Base Rate Book" (2016), "Measuring the Moat" (2016, with Callahan), "ROIC and the Investment Process", "Total Addressable Market" — research notes
- Columbia Business School: Security Analysis course materials

**Signature frameworks:**
- **Reverse DCF / price-implied expectations:** start from price, solve for the growth, margin, and competitive-advantage-period assumptions the market is embedding. The question is never "what is it worth?" first — it is "what must be true for today's price to be right, and how plausible is that against the historical distribution?"
- **Base rates:** sales growth, ROIC persistence, and margin expansion all have documented distributions by company size. A thesis requiring a €50bn company to compound revenue at 20% for a decade must cite how many companies of that size have ever done it.
- **ROIC decomposition:** ROIC = NOPAT margin × invested capital turnover; locate the moat in one of the two and explain its defensibility.
- **Competitive Advantage Period (CAP):** the number of years ROIC > WACC is sustained; the most under-examined input in any DCF.
- **Skill vs luck:** outcome quality ≠ decision quality; insist on process evaluation.

**Decision principles:**
- Expectations gaps, not cheap multiples, are the source of returns.
- Always quantify the outside view before trusting the inside view.
- Disaggregate growth into volume, price, and mix before believing it.

## 2. Valuation mechanics — Aswath Damodaran

**Check it owns:** Valuation referee. Audits every model for internal consistency; owns cost of capital.

**Published work:**
- *Investment Valuation* (3rd ed., 2012); *Narrative and Numbers* (2017); *The Little Book of Valuation* (2011) — academic/practitioner books
- NYU Stern data pages: equity risk premia (updated monthly), industry betas, country risk premia — living datasets; always pull the current values via search, never quote remembered ones
- Musings on Markets blog and annual company valuations — practitioner sources

**Signature frameworks:**
- **FCFF DCF with explicit consistency checks:** growth = reinvestment rate × return on capital. Any model where growth is assumed without the reinvestment to fund it is rejected. Terminal growth ≤ risk-free rate; terminal ROIC convergence toward WACC unless a durable moat is argued explicitly.
- **Story-to-numbers:** every input maps to a narrative claim ("this margin assumption means the company becomes as profitable as the best operator in the industry's history — say that out loud and defend it").
- **Cost of capital as a dynamic number:** bottom-up beta, current ERP, country risk for revenue-weighted geography.
- **Explicit uncertainty:** scenario trees or Monte Carlo on the 2–3 inputs that drive the value, rather than a single margin-of-safety haircut. He is on record that margin of safety is a blunt instrument compared to modeling the uncertainty directly — this is a standing collision with Klarman.
- **Corporate lifecycle:** the right model depends on where the firm sits (young growth: revenue-driven, survival-adjusted; mature: earnings-driven; declining: divestiture/liquidation value).

**Decision principles:**
- Price is what the market says; value is what the model defends; never confuse a pricing (multiples) with a valuation.
- The terminal value should be interrogated, not buried: report its share of total value.
- A valuation you cannot be argued out of is a religion, not an analysis.

## 3. Concentrated ownership — Li Lu

**Check it owns:** Conviction and sizing lead. Owns the circle-of-competence and holding-period tests.

**Published work:**
- *Civilization, Modernization, Value Investing and China* (2020) — practitioner book
- Columbia lecture (2006, in Greenwald's course) and Peking University lectures (2015, 2019) — transcribed lectures
- Himalaya Capital's public record: extreme concentration (historically a handful of positions; the BYD position held since 2002 is the canonical example) — verify current holdings via 13F in Phase 0; do not quote remembered positions

**Signature frameworks:**
- **Circle of competence as a hard gate:** if the analysis cannot explain the unit economics and the 10-year competitive landscape to a skeptic, the position size is zero regardless of apparent cheapness.
- **The ownership test:** would you buy the whole business at this price and hold it with no quotation for a decade? Sizing follows from the quality of the answer, not from the upside multiple.
- **Asymmetric research depth:** very few ideas, researched to the point of knowing the business better than most insiders; the willingness to do nothing for years.
- **Value evolves:** from Graham cigar butts to quality compounders — the intrinsic-value discipline is constant, the asset type is not.

**Decision principles:**
- Concentration is the rational response to rare genuine insight; diversification is the rational response to its absence. Most portfolios should be honest about which regime they are in.
- The biggest risk is not volatility but being wrong about the business; the second biggest is selling a great business early.
- Opportunity cost is the only benchmark: every holding must out-argue the best alternative, including cash.

## 4. Cycles and sentiment — Howard Marks

**Check it owns:** Sentiment-synthesis lead. Owns Phase 0 interpretation: what does the market believe, and where is the pendulum?

**Published work:**
- *The Most Important Thing* (2011); *Mastering the Market Cycle* (2018) — practitioner books
- Oaktree memos (1990–present) — the primary corpus; "On Bubble Watch", "The Race to the Bottom", "You Can't Predict. You Can Prepare." Pull recent memos via search when cycle positioning is material.

**Signature frameworks:**
- **Second-level thinking:** first-level says "great company, buy"; second-level says "great company, but everyone thinks so, so it's overpriced — sell." The view must be located relative to consensus, not in a vacuum.
- **Risk = probability of permanent capital loss,** not volatility. This is the philosophical root of the skill's Kelly-primary sizing doctrine.
- **The pendulum:** sentiment oscillates between euphoria and depression and spends little time at the happy medium; measure where it is via Phase 0 (valuation vs history, fund flows, short interest, narrative tone).
- **"I don't know" school:** macro forecasting is mostly futile; cycle *positioning* (aggressive vs defensive) is feasible. Calibrate offense/defense, don't predict.
- **The perversity of risk:** the riskiest moment is when risk is perceived as lowest.

**Decision principles:**
- You cannot simultaneously have above-average returns and consensus opinions.
- What matters is not what you buy but what you pay.
- When there's nothing clever to do, the mistake is trying to be clever.

## 5. Margin of safety — Seth Klarman

**Check it owns:** Bear-case stress-tester. Owns the downside-first analysis and the absolute-return discipline.

**Published work:**
- *Margin of Safety* (1991) — practitioner book (out of print; positions well documented)
- Baupost investor letters (excerpts public) and interviews/lectures (HBS, Ivey, *Financial Analysts Journal* pieces) — practitioner sources
- Preface to *Security Analysis* (6th ed., 2008)

**Signature frameworks:**
- **Margin of safety as the central concept:** buy at a sufficient discount to conservatively estimated value that being wrong on the estimate still avoids permanent loss.
- **Downside-first analysis:** the first question is never the upside; it is "how much can be lost, with what probability, and is that survivable at the proposed size?"
- **Skepticism of DCF precision:** long-horizon cash-flow forecasts compound small errors into large ones — prefer valuations anchored in nearer-term, harder numbers (asset value, liquidation value, private-market transaction value, current owner earnings) where available. Standing collision with Damodaran, who answers with explicit uncertainty modeling.
- **Catalysts:** value without a path to realization can stay cheap indefinitely; identify the catalyst or accept the time-arbitrage cost.
- **Cash is a position:** the absolute-return investor holds cash when nothing meets the bar; relative-return thinking ("fully invested because the benchmark is") is a category error.

**Decision principles:**
- Avoiding loss is the precondition for compounding, not a competing objective.
- The market is a place where securities are mispriced by forced and emotional sellers; ask who is selling and why.
- Complexity and illiquidity are sources of return only when they are the *reason* for the mispricing.

## 6. Quality compounders — Terry Smith

**Check it owns:** Quality gate and value-trap detector. Owns the ROCE/cash-conversion screen and turnover discipline.

**Published work:**
- *Investing for Growth* (2020); *Accounting for Growth* (1992) — practitioner books
- Fundsmith annual shareholder letters and Owner's Manual — primary corpus; pull the most recent letter via search when his current positioning matters

**Signature frameworks:**
- **The three-step doctrine:** buy good companies; don't overpay; do nothing.
- **"Good company" is quantified:** sustained ROCE materially above cost of capital, high gross margins (pricing power), high cash conversion (earnings are real), low capital intensity, interest cover that survives a downturn, growth funded internally.
- **Source-of-return arithmetic:** over long horizons, return converges on the business's ROIC plus/minus the multiple change — paying up modestly for durable high returns on capital beats buying mediocrity cheaply (with the explicit caveat that "modestly" is doing real work; he is not a quality-at-any-price investor and has criticized that reading).
- **Value-trap detection:** statistically cheap + structurally declining ROCE + capital-intensive turnaround story = trap. Cheapness is a fact about the past; quality is a claim about the future.
- **Turnover as a cost center:** trading is a tax on returns; the default action on a great business is inaction.

**Decision principles:**
- No amount of cleverness in entry price rescues a bad business held for a long time; time is the friend of the wonderful business and the enemy of the mediocre one.
- Beware accounting growth without cash: his original career was built on exposing it.
- Sell on thesis breach (ROCE deterioration, capital-allocation vandalism), not on price targets.

## 7. Contested register

These are real, durable splits between the lenses' published positions. When the checks land on opposite sides, both positions are reported in the verdict, never averaged.

| Axis | Side A | Side B | Handling |
|------|--------|--------|----------------|
| Handling valuation uncertainty | Damodaran: model it explicitly (scenarios, Monte Carlo) | Klarman: discount it bluntly (deep margin of safety, near-term hard numbers) | Report both the modeled distribution and the haircut-based entry price; flag when they disagree on whether current price is acceptable |
| Cheapness vs quality | Klarman: price discipline first; quality at fair price can still be a poor risk/reward | Smith / Li Lu: business quality first; cheap mediocrity decays | Run Smith's value-trap screen AND Klarman's downside math; a name must pass both or the split is reported |
| Concentration | Li Lu: concentrate on rare insight | Marks: humility about insight argues for more diversification and cycle-aware defense | Resolve via the sizing doctrine in `portfolio-optimization.md`: conviction evidence determines where on the Kelly fraction the position sits |
| DCF as primary tool | Damodaran / Mauboussin: yes, with discipline | Klarman / Smith: prefer harder, nearer numbers (asset value; current ROCE and cash conversion) | Deep Dives produce both a DCF-family value and a hard-numbers anchor; divergence > 30% triggers explicit reconciliation |
| Role of macro/cycle | Marks: positioning must reflect the cycle | Smith / Li Lu: ignore macro, own great businesses through cycles | Cycle view affects sizing and cash level, never the intrinsic-value estimate itself |
| Selling discipline | Smith / Li Lu: sell on thesis breach only | Klarman: sell as price approaches conservative value; recycle into wider gaps | Report the holding's position on both rules; if they conflict, present as explicit disagreement with the time-horizon assumption named |
